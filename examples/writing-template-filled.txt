It is often argued that narrowing the gap between the richest and poorest members of society is the best way to make people happier. While I accept that reducing inequality can improve financial security, I believe that it should be combined with better public services and stronger community support.

One reason for supporting this view is that additional income can make a substantial difference to households struggling to meet basic needs. This is because such families may spend most of their earnings on rent and food, leaving little money for healthcare or education. As a result, a modest increase in disposable income can help them cover essential expenses, which can in turn reduce anxiety about unexpected bills. For example, a family receiving targeted financial support might be able to pay for a child's necessary treatment without falling behind on rent. This illustrates how improving the living conditions of lower-income households can increase their sense of security.

However, income equality alone cannot address every source of unhappiness. Even if residents have similar earnings, they may still experience poor health or social isolation. For instance, older people in a neighbourhood without accessible healthcare or community activities could remain anxious and lonely despite relatively equal incomes. Therefore, governments should improve access to essential services and create opportunities for social contact, rather than treating a smaller income gap as a complete solution.

In conclusion, although reducing excessive inequality can make basic living costs more manageable, a happier society also depends on health, supportive relationships and reliable public services.
